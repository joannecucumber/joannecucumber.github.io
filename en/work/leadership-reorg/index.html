Team transformation

Giving role development a clear path

Keeping product-design depth as the foundation, while developing data, PM, or AI implementation skills according to the responsibility each role needs to carry.

Talent density depends on the scope of outcomes people can own

In developing CMoney’s design department, my focus has been the scope of problems the same team can take responsibility for, rather than the number of senior designers. When design only receives screen requests, problem framing, data interpretation, coordination, and implementation are split across roles. Design intent can become fragmented through that back-and-forth.

I therefore framed development around T-shaped capabilities: retain depth in product design and add the adjacent skills a task needs. Some roles need to move earlier into data and problem definition; others need to integrate PM responsibilities or build directly with AI. Role development should follow the outcomes a position needs to own, connecting expertise to the work.

Adjacent capabilities develop according to the project’s needs

Product-design depth remains shared; additional skills depend on the role

The professional foundation still includes understanding problems, flows and interactions, information presentation, and collaboration. From that foundation, the question is which capability a position needs next: interpreting data and forming a hypothesis, coordinating work across roles, or turning an idea into an interactive experience.

This shapes both existing roles and hiring. A vacancy first needs a clear problem to own, followed by the relevant additional capability and a realistic way to assess it. Adjacent skills must relate directly to the task; otherwise requirements accumulate without clarifying responsibility. AI Builder is therefore one option, rather than a template for every designer.

Data and PM skills move designers earlier into product decisions

One emerging change is designers participating in data analysis and turning observations into problems and design directions. Some role definitions also bring design and PM responsibilities together, including requirements, priorities, and coordination. Design enters earlier and can understand more fully why a proposal is worth pursuing.

This integration aims to reduce the cost of repeatedly passing requirements between functions. It does not transfer every PM responsibility to designers. Each position needs a defined scope: which decisions it owns and which remain shared. The purpose of developing adjacent skills is to help someone connect related parts of a problem.

AI implementation extends responsibility toward working outcomes

Another change involves designers working as AI Builders, extending delivery from design files to a working product. This approach has appeared in more than one project, creating an opportunity to assess whether individual experience can develop into a capability other designers can learn.

The value of extending downstream is bringing design intent into the experience more directly and identifying problems through interaction earlier. Professional boundaries remain important. Implementation responsibility needs to match the task and capabilities, with agreement on delivery and validation. PM, data, and technical responsibilities should not all be added to one person at once.

Roles are beginning to change; effectiveness must be tested through delivery

Designers have begun participating in data work and working as AI Builders. Some positions are being defined and recruited with combined design and PM responsibilities. T-shaped capabilities are also reflected in role expectations and actual hiring, helping the team discuss which kind of person a task needs rather than classify people only by seniority or tool proficiency.

These are signs of broader role ownership, rather than proof of an overall efficiency improvement. The next step is to compare delivery cycles, handoffs, and output quality, and relate hiring expectations to collaboration after someone joins. Whether talent density has improved ultimately depends on how fully the team can take responsibility for problems and outcomes.
