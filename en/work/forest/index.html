Growth design

Getting new users to stay — and to pay: Forest's sign-up, mission system, and subscription redesign

Led the redesign of the sign-up flow, the mission system, and the subscription flow: significantly lifted sign-up conversion, gave users a reason to come back every day, and the subscription redesign drove revenue growth.

Company / Product
Seekrtech (Forest)
Role
Senior Product Designer
Period
2024/03 – 2025/04
Tools
Figma, Rive

Images are screenshots of the Figma working files (UI labels in Chinese). Prices, internal string keys, colleague names, account emails, and internal sticky notes are covered with solid dark blocks.

Background

  • Forest is a globally known focus and productivity app. As Senior Product Designer at Seekrtech (Mar 2024 – Apr 2025), I owned design for user growth, engagement, and subscriptions.
  • The company was rolling out a unified account system across its products, so Forest’s sign-up and login had to migrate to it, and existing users had to link their accounts.
  • At the same time, Forest was switching from a one-time purchase model to a subscription model, so the entire payment flow had to be redesigned.
  • The product team worked in sprints, with spec and AC (acceptance criteria) documents for each.

Problem

  1. Low sign-up conversion: only a small share of new users registered. Without accounts, the product struggled to sync data, retain users, and lead them to purchase.
  2. Users couldn’t tell sign-up from login: support reports showed users confusing “sign up” and “log in” during third-party login.
  3. No reason to come back every day: beyond the core focus feature, there was no ongoing incentive to return. The mission system’s stated goals were to “build user habits, increase stickiness, and guide users to experience the core features.”
  4. Subscription flow: see “Subscription Flow Redesign” below.

My Role

Senior Product Designer. Led the redesign of the sign-up flow, the mission system, and the subscription flow.

Process

Research

  • Compiled support reports and user pain points (sign-up/login confusion), and mapped every scenario for new and existing account holders: single account, multiple accounts, and failed linking.
  • For the mission system, studied Duolingo’s quest mechanics and compared two reward designs: predictable rewards vs. tiered “blind box” rewards.

Define

  • Sign-up incentive: a “sign-up gift pack” (a trial of paid features) lets users feel Premium’s value first. Tracked metrics: new-user registration conversion and first-purchase conversion.
  • Trigger timing: prompts appear when users need the feature, e.g., tapping a specific tree species, setting a longer focus session, or opening advanced analytics.
  • A three-tier mission system:
    • Daily missions: build daily engagement
    • Daily check-ins: return visits across multiple time slots
    • Monthly challenges: daily missions add up to long-term rewards
  • Modular configuration, reusable and flexible.

Design

  • Sign-up and login flow:
    • Inline field validation, with all error states consolidated into one overview.
    • Users who drop off mid-flow can resume directly at the email verification step.
    • Third-party login options adapt to the user’s platform.
    • Visual cues make it clear that “this is the same account,” reducing confusion.
  • Legacy account linking:
    • UI and copy avoid making users think they are “creating a new account.”
    • Before linking, clearly show which account will be linked; after success, explain how to log in going forward.
    • If linking fails, guide users to customer support.
  • Purchase without signing up: removed the “sign up first” paywall; after purchase, users are prompted to register, with a note that an account protects their purchase history.
  • Mission system:
    • Defined UI and rules for every card state: not started, in progress, claimable, cooling down, claimed.
    • Built the reward-claim animation with a Rive state machine.
    • Placed the entry point in the side menu.
    • Covered the iPad version and edge cases.

Validate

  • Accepted work in stages against each sprint demo scope, and resolved edge cases one by one (e.g., retry design for network errors).

Subscription Flow Redesign

Problem

  • Forest was moving from a one-time purchase model to subscriptions. The entire payment flow (entry points, plan page, trial, payment, restore purchase, and cancellation) had to be redesigned for the subscription model, and I led the redesign.
  • Users needed to see at a glance what a subscription gives them and how it differs from the free version. User stories from the Figma specs:
    • “I just want to quickly see what I get with a subscription, but there’s so much explanation that I don’t get it.”
    • “I’m not sure whether this is subscription-only. A direct comparison would help.”

What I Did

  • End-to-end subscription flow:
    • Entry from the home screen, account page, side menu, and other touchpoints.
    • Routing by account and registration status (registered or not; unified-account member or not).
    • Completion of a trial or subscription.
    • Coverage of subscription success, subscription failure (unstable network or invalid payment method, each with its own recovery CTA), restore purchase, trial reminders, and plan changes and cancellation.
  • Restore purchase: checks whether the Apple ID has an active order and which account it is tied to, then guides users to either link it to the current account or log in with the original account; on failure, users are directed to the FAQ or support.
  • Subscription entry points and placement: planned entry points on the home screen, side menu, and account page, and how screens differ before and after subscribing.
  • Subscription page and plan selection:
    • Two subscription page layouts, for Free and Premium users.
    • Width rules for phone and iPad in portrait and landscape.
    • Content scope split between native and web.
    • Component specs for plan cards and plan selection (corner radius, borders, line counts, and text truncation rules).
    • Built the subscription page skeleton and designed its animation: subscriber-exclusive content such as the seasonal tree species as the key visual, with animation safe areas marked.
  • Displaying subscriber-exclusive content: tree species and music appear dimmed with a tag (not selectable) before subscribing and display normally afterwards; tapping unowned exclusive content opens a choice between a one-time purchase and a subscription. Features keep the existing paywall screen, which leads to the subscription page.
  • One-time purchase audit: audited the mechanics and copy of the original one-time purchase model (Pro version) and flagged what had to change for the move to subscriptions.
  • Segmented offers (Sprints 12–13):
    • Users who never trialed: an extended free trial, as a one-time, time-limited offer with a countdown.
    • Users who trialed but aren’t subscribed: a time-limited discount plan.
    • Users who cancelled during a trial: a time-limited discount plan.
    • Defined start and stop conditions for each segment; after any error handling in the subscription flow, users always return to the offer page.
    • Designed a subscription survey to learn what matters to users who haven’t subscribed yet.
  • Subscription walkthrough (Sprint 14):
    • Before an actual trial starts, every entry point leads to the walkthrough first.
    • Gives equal weight to advanced features and new exclusive content.
    • Clearly lists subscription benefits and cancellation rules, and explains that users can cancel anytime.
    • Separates explanations for Free and Premium users, grounded in the Fogg Behavior Model and a MECE breakdown of free users’ pain points.

Results

Led the subscription flow redesign, driving revenue growth.

Results

  • Led the sign-up flow redesign, significantly lifting sign-up conversion.
  • Led the mission system optimization, giving users a reason to come back every day.
  • Led the subscription flow redesign, driving revenue growth.

Reflection

  • When users don’t pay, it’s often not that they don’t want to; they just aren’t sure yet what they’ll get. Making the benefits and how to cancel clear, and letting users try it and feel the value first, does more to win payment than blocking them at the entry point.
