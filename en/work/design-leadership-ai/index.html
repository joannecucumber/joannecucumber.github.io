Leadership overview

Setting direction while developing the team’s judgment

Connecting portfolio review, role expectations, and realistic scenarios so both the team and the candidate can assess judgment, collaboration, and fit.

Collaboration needs to be assessed in the context of the work

Since 2025, I have led a design department of more than 30 people at CMoney. One important management change has been reconsidering how we assess whether someone fits the team. Hiring had focused on professional skills, past experience, and values. Those are useful signals, but they do not fully reveal how someone will work with changing requirements, conflicting opinions, and practical constraints.

A portfolio shows completed work, while an interview reveals how a person describes their experience. Neither easily shows their response to a disagreement as it unfolds. I therefore made the process a two-way assessment: the team observes the candidate’s judgment, and the candidate experiences the working context. The question extends from design proficiency to whether both sides can work together in that setting.

Hiring now gives both sides a chance to test the fit

Expectations are discussed before the onsite interview

The phone conversation explains the work, collaboration, and role development while exploring the candidate’s motivation and expectations. Both sides can discuss differences in how they understand autonomy, cross-functional work, and responsibility before reaching a later stage.

This also changes how a manager presents a vacancy. Alongside the required skills, the conversation needs to explain the problems the role will own. The professional baseline stays consistent, while specific expectations reflect the team and task. Candidates then have information to decide whether this is work they want to take on.

Realistic scenarios make design judgment observable

The onsite interview begins with the portfolio and past experience, then introduces a scenario close to the work. Product-design scenarios bring in goals, data, and constraints. They reveal how a candidate clarifies the problem, prioritizes, and adapts a proposal when conditions change. The reasoning matters alongside the final answer.

Visual-design scenarios introduce differing stakeholder views. A candidate first chooses a proposal, then interviewers take marketing and editorial perspectives and offer different, sometimes conflicting, requests. This brings professional explanation, requirements integration, and communication into the same exercise. Candidates also get a practical sense of that collaboration.

The decision considers both capability and willingness to take responsibility

The assessment combines the portfolio, decisions made during the exercise, and communication. Interviewers need to understand how a person gathers information, explains trade-offs, and responds to other perspectives. Candidates need to understand how the team discusses design, what disagreement looks like, and what responsibility they would carry.

I preserve this two-way choice because having the capability and sharing expectations about the work are separate questions. Standards should help both sides decide, rather than simply add more assessment stages. This reflects a broader leadership principle: make responsibility clear, then find the fit between expertise, individual motivation, and the role.

The revised process provides a fuller basis for judging fit

Scenario-based assessment and explicit discussion of work expectations are now part of the hiring process. The available evidence extends beyond portfolios and past experience to candidates’ decisions, responses, and collaboration in the moment. Candidates can also understand the working context before joining.

This is a change in assessment, rather than evidence of improved retention or hiring outcomes. Interview observations still need to be compared with delivery and collaboration after someone joins, so questions and standards can be refined. The management goal is to bring hiring closer to the actual work and support informed decisions on both sides.
