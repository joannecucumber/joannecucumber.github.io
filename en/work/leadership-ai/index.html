AI adoption

Turning exploration into a team capability

Adapting collaboration to project starting points, technical dependencies, and risk, while supporting independent exploration and shared learning.

AI has changed where projects start and where work is handed over

While leading AI adoption in CMoney’s design department, I found that projects did not all start at the same point. Some product managers had already built an initial version with AI-assisted coding and needed design refinement. Other tasks involved designers building working products directly. Content work faced a different obstacle: frequent production and dependencies across roles. Designers had also formed their own practical learning group to share exploration and implementation experience.

A fixed sequence of requirements, design files, and engineering could not address all these situations equally well. My starting point was to ask what already existed and what the project needed next, then decide how design should participate. The quality baseline remains shared, while who builds the prototype, when design enters, and what it delivers can vary by project.

Different projects need different forms of design participation

A PM-built first version shifts design toward assessment and refinement

Some projects come to design with an AI-built first version from the product manager and a request to improve it. Working material exists before design begins. Designers need to understand which needs it addresses, where the experience needs attention, and which parts should be retained, changed, or reconsidered.

The first version gives the PM and designer something concrete to examine together. Design contributes by identifying problems, integrating requirements, and improving quality. The responsibility is to develop the product from that starting point, rather than merely reproduce existing screens as a new set of design files.

Project risk determines the scope of direct implementation

Designers using AI-assisted coding to turn ideas into interactive work is an established situation. But a new idea suited to rapid validation and a major change with existing architecture and complex dependencies have different delivery requirements. Understanding technical conditions and risk comes first, followed by deciding how far direct implementation should go and where design and engineering responsibilities remain distinct. This connects the speed advantage to the responsibilities of formal delivery.

Broader responsibility also brings a learning burden: designers need to combine product thinking, interaction design, and tool use. They cannot all be expected to make the transition at the same speed. Practical peer learning has already emerged. The next leadership task is to gradually capture cases, suitable conditions, and difficulties as reusable experience, giving new approaches support while retaining room for different tasks and specialties.

Automation reduces waiting in frequent content production

Content and social work face another kind of dependency: frequent output with copy, visuals, and scheduling spread across different steps. Established applications include generating audience-specific copy within design and connecting content, visual production, and publishing through automated workflows.

Here, the important change is how dependencies are arranged. Designers can obtain more of what they need independently, connect repetitive steps, and focus attention on the audience, content, and quality. Performance data collection and subsequent content decisions still need further work. Completed production does not establish that the entire operational cycle is in place.

Adoption is changing collaboration and delivery; quality still needs evaluation

PM-built initial versions have generated requests for design refinement. There are also examples of designers implementing directly, automating frequent production, and organizing their own practical learning. AI adoption is beginning to change how projects divide responsibility, where handoffs occur, and how far design delivery extends. Shared methods and collaboration boundaries are still being developed.

The next questions are whether each approach improves quality and reduces rework in its own context, and whether others can understand and reuse the experience. Independent peer learning has already begun; sustained sharing and knowledge development remain ongoing. Individual cases are not treated as proof of department-wide capability or business impact. Different approaches still face common questions: are responsibilities clear, is quality maintained, and does the complete task move forward more effectively?
